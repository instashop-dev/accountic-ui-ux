## DPDP Act 2023: Client Consent Framework for CA Engagement Letters

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 is no longer a distant regulatory event. With enforcement rules expected in the first half of 2025, every CA firm that collects client PAN numbers, Aadhaar copies, bank statements, or even a mobile number is already sitting inside the Act's compliance perimeter — whether or not the engagement letter reflects that reality.

---

## Why DPDP Act 2023 Changes Everything for CA Engagement Letters

The DPDP Act received Presidential assent in August 2023, and the Government has been working through the rule-making process since. Most practitioners have watched from the sidelines, assuming the Act targets large technology companies. That assumption is wrong and expensive.

The moment your firm collects a client's PAN, Aadhaar, financial statements, or contact details, you become a **Data Fiduciary** under Section 2(i) of the Act. That status carries legally enforceable obligations around consent, purpose limitation, and data security — obligations that attach from the point of collection, not from the date you sign a compliance checklist.

Here's the practical problem: every standard engagement letter template in circulation — including ICAI's sample formats — was drafted before DPDP existed. They contain no valid consent language, no purpose specification, and no disclosure of third-party sharing. From a DPDP standpoint, your existing onboarding process creates liability from day one of enforcement.

The window to fix this is now, before the rules are notified and the Data Protection Board begins exercising its adjudicatory powers.

---

## Key DPDP Definitions Every CA Must Know Before Drafting Consent

Before you touch a single clause in your engagement letter, you need to understand how DPDP terminology maps onto your actual practice. The definitions are not abstract — they determine whether you need explicit consent or can rely on implied lawful purpose.

| DPDP Term | What It Means for Your CA Practice | Practical Implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Data Fiduciary** | Your firm when it decides *why* and *how* client personal data is processed (e.g., for filing returns, conducting audits, or providing advisory) | You carry the primary consent and security obligations — cannot delegate accountability to a client |
| **Data Processor** | Your firm when processing data *on behalf of another fiduciary* (e.g., when you handle payroll data for a corporate client who is itself the fiduciary for its employees) | You still need a Data Processing Agreement but the consent obligation sits with the fiduciary you serve |
| **Data Principal** | Your individual client, or an individual whose data your corporate client shares with you (employees, directors, guarantors) | They hold the right to access, correct, and erase their data — your engagement letter must acknowledge this |
| **Consent Manager** | An DPDP Board-registered intermediary through whom consent can be given, tracked, and withdrawn | Not mandatory today for CA engagements, but worth watching as the ecosystem matures |
| **Lawful Purpose / Legitimate Use** | Processing without explicit consent is permitted only for narrowly defined state functions and certain compliance obligations | Most CA services — tax advisory, secretarial, valuation — will require explicit, purpose-specific consent |
| **Special Category Data** | Not yet exhaustively defined in the Act, but Aadhaar, health data, and financial distress indicators almost certainly qualify under the rules being drafted | Higher care standards apply — do not process without clearly scoped consent and security controls |

The fiduciary-versus-processor distinction matters most when you run a shared services model or outsource work. In many engagements, your firm is simultaneously a fiduciary for your own client relationship and a processor for your corporate client's employee data. Your engagement letter needs to address both postures separately.

## Anatomy of a DPDP-Compliant Consent Clause for Engagement Letters

Consider how Rajesh Mehta & Associates, a mid-sized CA firm in Pune, approached this challenge in January 2025. Their old template had a single line: *"The client agrees to provide all documents and information required for the engagement."* Under DPDP, that sentence is not consent — it is an instruction to the client, not an informed agreement by the client.

Their updated consent block, embedded as a standalone section titled "Personal Data Consent," contains seven core disclosures:

**1. Identity of the Data Fiduciary** — The firm's full legal name, registered address, and a named point of contact. Not just "the firm."

**2. Purpose of Processing** — Specific and bounded. "To prepare and file income tax returns for Assessment Year 2025-26, to conduct statutory audit under the Companies Act, and to provide related GST advisory" — not "for accounting and tax purposes." Each engagement type should be listed separately.

**3. Categories of Personal Data Collected** — PAN, Aadhaar number, bank account details, director identification numbers, financial statements, and any health or insurance data relevant to the engagement.

**4. Retention Period** — "Personal data will be retained for a period of eight years from the close of the relevant assessment year, in accordance with obligations under the Income Tax Act, 1961, and thereafter securely deleted or anonymised." Firms that cannot name a retention period are not compliant.

**5. Third-Party Sharing** — Every category of recipient, not vague language. Cloud accounting platforms, government portals (Income Tax Portal, MCA21), bank confirmation services, and any outsourced bookkeeping vendors must each be named by category.

**6. Withdrawal Mechanism** — A specific email address or written process through which the client can withdraw consent, with the firm's committed response timeline. "You may withdraw your consent at any time by writing to privacy@rajeshmehtaca.com and we will acknowledge within 5 business days."

**7. Grievance Contact** — A named Grievance Officer with contact details, as DPDP requires a formal grievance redressal mechanism.

The DPDP's "clear and plain language" standard means dense legal prose will not hold up. If a reasonably educated client cannot understand what they are consenting to, the consent is challengeable. Rajesh Mehta's team ran their draft through a plain-language review before finalising — a step every firm should replicate.

Critically, **bundled consent is invalid**. You cannot have one checkbox that covers tax filing, audit, advisory, and data sharing with third parties simultaneously. Each distinct purpose requires a separate, affirmative consent mechanism. In a digital engagement letter, this means separate consent checkboxes. In a paper format, it means separate signature fields against each purpose block.

## Step-by-Step Workflow to Embed Consent Into Your Existing Engagement Letter Process

1. Pull every engagement letter template currently in use across your practice — statutory audit, tax representation, GST compliance, secretarial, and advisory mandates. List them in a master inventory. Most firms discover they have five to twelve distinct templates, many of which have never been formally reviewed since the partner who drafted them retired.

2. For each template, map every category of personal data you collect during onboarding and throughout the engagement. Go beyond the obvious — include WhatsApp communications, scanned documents stored in email, and data fields captured in your practice management software.

3. Identify which data categories constitute personal data under DPDP and flag any that may qualify as special category data (Aadhaar, health records, financial distress indicators). These need elevated handling and explicit consent — they cannot be bundled with routine tax data.

4. Draft the consent clause using the seven-element structure described above. Write a separate consent block for each distinct engagement type. Have your draft reviewed by a lawyer with DPDP exposure — this is not a step to skip, given the penalty quantum involved.

5. Embed the consent block into your digital engagement letter format as a distinct, clearly labelled section. If you use DocuSign, Adobe Sign, or any Indian e-signature platform, configure separate signature or acknowledgement fields for each consent purpose. If you use paper letters, add distinct initials fields.

6. Update your client onboarding checklist to make signed consent a mandatory gate before any data collection begins. Reception staff and article clerks who handle new client documents need to be briefed on this requirement — the process fails if the consent is gathered after data has already been processed.

7. Obtain time-stamped consent for every new client from the date your updated process goes live. For existing clients, issue a DPDP consent update notice — a short letter or email that presents the consent clause and invites affirmative agreement, ideally before enforcement rules are notified.

8. Store consent proof in a dedicated consent register — client name, date of consent, engagement type, version number of the consent language used, and the medium through which consent was given (email, e-signature, physical signature). This register is your primary exhibit if the Data Protection Board ever investigates.

9. Build a written SOP for handling consent withdrawal requests. The SOP should define who receives the withdrawal request, who acknowledges it, and who is responsible for executing any consequential data deletion — subject to mandatory statutory retention carve-outs discussed below.

## Third-Party Data Sharing: Consent Obligations When Using Outsourced Staff or Cloud Tools

This is the section most CA firms get wrong, because the problem is invisible until a client asks a direct question: "Who else has access to my financial data?"

DPDP requires you to disclose every **category** of third-party recipient in your consent clause, and naming "our software vendors" or "service providers" is explicitly insufficient. The Act's purpose-limitation principle means clients must understand, at the time of consent, which types of entities will receive their data and why.

For a typical mid-size CA firm in 2025, the third-party list includes: the Income Tax Portal and MCA21 (government portals), cloud accounting platforms such as Tally Prime on Cloud, Zoho Books, or QuickBooks Online, GST filing software and API-linked portals, e-signature platforms, article clerks and outsourced bookkeepers (including offshore processing arrangements), and any AI-assisted tools used for data extraction or analysis. Each must be named by category in the consent clause.

Before you share client data with any of these sub-processors, you must have a **Data Processing Agreement (DPA)** in place with that vendor. A DPA governs what data is shared, for what purpose, with what security standards, and what happens to the data at the end of the engagement. Most Indian SaaS vendors do not yet proactively offer DPAs — you will need to request them, and in some cases negotiate the terms.

If you use any offshore resource — a bookkeeper in the Philippines, a data entry team in Sri Lanka, or a KPO in Bengaluru processing work for an overseas CA firm — the DPDP cross-border data transfer rules will apply. The rules are still being finalised, but assume that offshore sharing will require either explicit client consent or transfer to a country whitelisted by the Central Government. Structure your consent clause to cover offshore processing explicitly, so you are not forced to retroactively renegotiate client agreements when the cross-border rules are notified.

## Handling Consent Withdrawal and Data Deletion Requests from Clients

A high-net-worth individual for whom your firm handles personal tax and investment advisory terminates the engagement in March 2025 and sends a formal email invoking their DPDP right to withdraw consent and requesting deletion of all personal data your firm holds.

What happens next?

Your engagement letter, if updated correctly, will already contain the answer to this question. The letter should specify your firm's response timeline (DPDP says "as soon as reasonably practicable" — most practitioners are interpreting this as within 30 days for acknowledgement and 60 days for execution), the process for receiving and logging the request, and — critically — the carve-outs.

The carve-out language is essential. A CA firm cannot simply delete everything a client requests, because the Income Tax Act, 1961 requires retention of books and records for up to eight years, and the Companies Act, 2013 has its own audit documentation requirements. The engagement letter must pre-disclose this tension:

*"You may withdraw your consent at any time by writing to [privacy contact]. We will cease processing your personal data for purposes to which consent has been withdrawn. However, we are required by law to retain certain data — including records relevant to income tax assessments and statutory audit documentation — for the periods mandated under the Income Tax Act, 1961 and the Companies Act, 2013. Such data will be retained solely for statutory compliance purposes and will not be used for any other processing."*

This language does two things: it honours the client's DPDP right in principle, and it establishes the statutory-retention exception as a disclosed, pre-agreed carve-out. Without this language in your engagement letter, a client could argue that your continued retention of their data after a withdrawal request is a DPDP violation.

Build a withdrawal request log into your consent register. Every withdrawal request — date received, action taken, data deleted, data retained with statutory justification — needs to be documented, because the Data Protection Board can ask to see it.

## Penalties, Audit Readiness, and What a DPDP-Ready Consent Register Looks Like

The penalty framework under the DPDP Act is not scaled to firm size. A proprietor CA practice faces the same potential penalty quantum as a Big Four firm — up to **₹250 crore per breach instance** for failure to implement reasonable security safeguards, and up to **₹50 crore for non-fulfilment of consent obligations**. These are not theoretical figures; they are the ceiling the Data Protection Board is empowered to impose.

The practical defence against these penalties is documentation. Regulators in every jurisdiction — and the Data Protection Board will be no different — look first at whether you had a system and whether you followed it. A well-maintained consent register, even if imperfect, demonstrates good faith. No register at all demonstrates recklessness.

Your consent register should contain, at minimum, the following fields for each client and each engagement:

- Full name and identifier of the Data Principal (client or individual whose data is processed)
- Date and method of consent collection (e-signature, physical signature, email confirmation)
- Version number and date of the consent language used — this matters because your consent clause will evolve as rules are updated
- Specific purposes consented to, listed individually
- List of third-party categories disclosed at time of consent
- Any subsequent withdrawal events, with dates and actions taken
- Retention schedule applied to the client's data

Maintaining this register manually across a large client base is operationally unsustainable. ## Your Next Step as a CA

Do not wait for the DPDP Rules to be formally notified before you act. Enforcement can begin within weeks of notification, and retrofitting consent across an existing client base under time pressure is far harder than updating your process now.

This week, pull your most-used engagement letter template, run it against the seven-element consent checklist in this article, and identify every gap. If you have a digital client onboarding process, test whether it captures purpose-specific consent as a mandatory step. If you use any cloud tool or outsourced resource, check whether you have a DPA in place.

Firms that treat [DPDP compliance](/blog/dpdp-act-law-enforcement-data-disclosure-request/) as an engagement letter update project — not a technology project, not a legal team project — will implement it faster and more durably than those waiting for a perfect solution.
